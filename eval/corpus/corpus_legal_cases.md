## Case 1: Thornton v. Bellweather Industries (2019)

### Background

In 2017, Margaret Thornton, a quality assurance engineer at Bellweather Industries (a mid-size chemical manufacturer in Dayton, Ohio), reported to the company's internal compliance hotline that the factory was dumping untreated wastewater into the Linden Creek watershed. When the company took no action within 90 days, Thornton reported the violations to the Environmental Compliance Agency (ECA). Two weeks after the ECA opened an investigation, Thornton was terminated for "performance reasons."

### Legal Proceedings

Thornton filed suit in the Federal District Court for the Southern District of Ohio, alleging wrongful termination in violation of the Whistleblower Protection Statute (WPS), Section 214(b). Bellweather argued that Thornton's termination was based on documented performance issues predating her complaint and that the WPS only protects disclosures made directly to government agencies, not internal hotline reports.

Judge Patricia Devereaux presided. The trial lasted 11 days.

### Ruling

Judge Devereaux ruled in favor of Thornton, finding that the temporal proximity between the ECA report and termination (14 days) established a prima facie case of retaliation. The court rejected Bellweather's argument about the scope of WPS protection, holding that internal disclosures made in good faith also qualify if they are followed by a subsequent external report.

Thornton was awarded $340,000 in back pay, $150,000 in compensatory damages, and $75,000 in attorney's fees. Bellweather was also ordered to pay a $1.2 million fine to the ECA for the wastewater violations.

### Significance

The case established the "sequential disclosure doctrine" — the principle that internal complaints gain WPS protection retroactively when they lead to a qualifying external disclosure. The ruling has been cited in 38 subsequent whistleblower cases across six federal districts.

## Case 2: City of Ashford v. Luminos Energy Corp. (2021)

### Background

In 2020, the City of Ashford (population 92,000) filed suit against Luminos Energy Corp., a regional utility company, alleging that Luminos had systematically overcharged residential customers by applying industrial-rate demand charges to household accounts. An independent audit commissioned by the city found that approximately 14,200 households had been overcharged by an average of $23 per month over a period of 4 years (2016–2020), totaling an estimated $15.7 million in excess charges.

### Legal Proceedings

The case was heard in the Ashford County Superior Court before Judge Samuel Ngata. Luminos argued that the billing discrepancy was caused by a software migration error during a system upgrade in 2016 and was not intentional. The company offered to refund the overcharges but disputed the city's claim for punitive damages and penalties.

### Ruling

Judge Ngata found Luminos liable for unjust enrichment and negligent billing practices. He ordered full restitution of $15.7 million to affected households plus statutory interest of 4.5% per year, bringing the total restitution to $18.3 million. The court declined to award punitive damages, accepting Luminos's argument that the overcharges were the result of negligence rather than fraud.

However, the court imposed a $2 million civil penalty, citing Luminos's failure to detect the error for 4 years despite receiving 340 customer complaints during that period. The judge noted that "a pattern of unresolved complaints is, at minimum, evidence of institutional indifference."

### Settlement of Related Claims

Following the ruling, three neighboring municipalities (Branford, Kelton, and Ridgecove) filed similar claims against Luminos. All three were settled out of court in 2022 for a combined $6.1 million.

## Case 3: Rivera v. Stonewall Academy (2022)

### Background

In 2021, Daniel Rivera, a 16-year-old student at Stonewall Academy (a private secondary school in Portland, Oregon), was expelled for "repeated violations of the student code of conduct" after organizing a petition signed by 120 students demanding that the school add a mental health counselor to the staff. The school's headmaster, Dr. Lewis Cartwright, characterized the petition as "disruptive to school order."

Rivera's parents filed suit, alleging that the expulsion violated the state's Student Free Expression Act (SFEA), Section 7, which protects non-disruptive student speech at private institutions receiving state funding. Stonewall Academy received $280,000 in state education voucher payments in the 2020–2021 school year.

### Legal Proceedings

The case was heard in the Multnomah County Circuit Court before Judge Ana Reyes. Stonewall Academy argued that as a private institution, it retained the right to enforce its code of conduct and that the SFEA's definition of "non-disruptive" was unconstitutionally vague.

### Ruling

Judge Reyes ruled in favor of Rivera. The court found that circulating a petition was squarely within the SFEA's protection and that the school failed to demonstrate any actual disruption to educational activities. The vagueness challenge was rejected, with the court noting that the SFEA had been in force for 8 years and upheld in three prior cases.

The court ordered Rivera's reinstatement and expungement of disciplinary records. Stonewall Academy was ordered to pay $85,000 in damages and $40,000 in attorney's fees. The school was also required to revise its code of conduct within 120 days to comply with the SFEA.

### Aftermath

Dr. Cartwright resigned as headmaster in early 2023. The case prompted the state legislature to amend the SFEA to require all voucher-receiving schools to include an explicit free expression policy in their student handbooks, effective August 2023.

## Case 4: Patel v. QuickDeliver Inc. (2023)

### Background

Arjun Patel, a delivery driver for QuickDeliver Inc. (a gig-economy food delivery platform), filed a class action suit on behalf of approximately 8,500 drivers in the state of Illinois. The suit alleged that QuickDeliver misclassified drivers as independent contractors rather than employees, thereby denying them minimum wage protections, overtime pay, and benefits.

### Key Evidence

Patel's legal team presented evidence that QuickDeliver controlled drivers' routes, set delivery time targets, imposed penalties for declined orders (reducing a driver's "reliability score"), and required drivers to wear branded uniforms. Internal emails from QuickDeliver's COO, revealed during discovery, stated: "We need to keep the contractor label to avoid benefits costs. Structure the app controls to look optional on paper."

### Ruling

The Illinois Superior Court, per Judge Franklin Oduya, ruled that the drivers were employees under the state's ABC employment test. The court found that QuickDeliver failed all three prongs: the drivers were under the company's control (Prong A), the delivery work was the company's core business (Prong B), and drivers were not independently established in the delivery trade (Prong C).

QuickDeliver was ordered to pay $22.4 million in back wages and benefits to the class members, plus a $3 million civil penalty. The company announced it would appeal.

### Industry Impact

Following the ruling, two other gig-economy platforms in Illinois (SprintEats and ParkourLogistics) preemptively reclassified their drivers as employees. The case is widely regarded as one of the most consequential gig-worker classification rulings in the Midwest.
